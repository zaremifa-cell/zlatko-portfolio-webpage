01 — CONTEXT & PROBLEM

A discrepancy is easy to flag. A decision needs context.

AI tools are good at spotting that two legal documents disagree. The hard part comes after: the reviewer still has to understand the finding, check the evidence and make a decision they can defend to a partner. A highlighted sentence does not say which source should win.

I built the concept around one concrete case. In the fictional Northgate Growth Fund III, clause 3.1 of the term sheet says ten years; Article 2.1 of the draft Limited Partnership Agreement says eight. In this scenario the term sheet is the agreed reference.

My starting point: when documents, AI suggestions and colleagues’ comments live in separate places, the reason behind a change gets lost. The workspace keeps all three in view while keeping their roles clearly apart.

02 — REVIEWER & SCOPE

One reviewer, one conflict, one complete handoff.

The user is a legal associate preparing a draft for partner review. Their job is to fix the discrepancy without losing the original wording, its source or the history of the change.

I deliberately designed one task end to end instead of a broad feature set: open the document, inspect the finding, compare the two clauses, accept or edit the amendment, review the summary and send it for approval. A narrow scope let me design every state of that flow — including rejection, undo and handoff — rather than only its happy path.

Upload, clause generation, permissions and onboarding are out of scope. All funds, people and documents are fictional.

03 — INFORMATION HIERARCHY

Keep the document central and the finding in context.

Three areas: section navigation on the left, the document in the middle, the review panel on the right. The document stays the main reading surface; the panel only explains why the selected clause needs attention.

Selecting Article 2.1 connects the text to its finding. I gave AI suggestions and human comments visibly different treatments — the human comment carries a name and firm — so the reviewer always knows whether they are reading a machine’s suggestion or a person’s feedback before acting on it.

04 — SOURCE COMPARISON

Show what disagrees before asking for acceptance.

The comparison step puts the term-sheet clause and the LPA clause side by side: where each excerpt comes from, what duration it states and exactly which words would change. The reviewer should be able to explain the conflict without trusting the AI label.

I left out a confidence percentage on purpose. A number like 94% looks reassuring, but it says nothing about whether the source applies or whether the new wording is legally right. The label states the basis instead: “Conflicts with term sheet · Review required”.

Accept, Edit and Reject sit next to the proposed amendment. The rejection form opens empty rather than pre-filled by AI, so the reason is the reviewer’s own. Rejecting keeps the issue open — declining one suggestion does not resolve the disagreement.

05 — DECISIONS & RECOVERY

Make the change traceable and recoverable.

Accepting records who decided and when. Added and Removed are written as text, not only shown in color, so the change reads correctly for everyone.

A saved-change notice stays for ten seconds, but Undo does not disappear with it: it lives in the decision history, and using it returns the finding to Open.

One distinction runs through the whole flow: an accepted suggestion is not an approved document. The card names E. Markova as the person who accepted the amendment while keeping its AI origin, and the draft stays a draft until the partner signs off.

06 — APPROVAL HANDOFF

Summarize the decision before sending the draft.

The summary is a last check: accepted changes, open findings and a short note for the partner. Send for approval ends on a confirmation that names the partner and states that the document is awaiting review — the task closes, but the next responsibility stays visible.

07 — FIGMA FOUNDATIONS

Build the states from a small, editable system.

The file separates Foundations, Components and Screens. Five core screens form the main flow; editing, rejection and confirmation are supporting prototype states. Its foundations include 52 primitive and semantic variables and seven text styles. Semantic roles distinguish page backgrounds, surfaces, primary and secondary text, borders, actions and review statuses, so the screens share the same visual decisions.

Five component families cover buttons, status tags, diff chips, human comment cards and AI suggestion cards. Across 29 variants, these components express changes in size, emphasis and state. Auto Layout, component instances and variable bindings keep the deliverable editable rather than flattening it into screenshots.

The neutral document surface supports sustained reading, while a restrained accent identifies actions. AI cards have an explicit label and a left-side marker; human comments carry authorship. The purpose of these choices is to make the source and meaning of an item legible without adding competing decoration.

08 — MY ROLE & TOOLS

Design decisions mine; production accelerated with AI.

I defined the problem, the reviewer, the scope of the task and the interaction rules described on this page: how AI and human input are separated, why there is no confidence score, how rejection and undo behave and where acceptance ends and approval begins.

I used Claude for early research and copy drafts, and Codex to generate the Figma layers, components and variables from that specification. I then corrected and refined the file by hand in Figma and walked the full prototype from the document list to the approval confirmation.

The concept has not yet been tested with legal reviewers. The plan for that is below.

09 — NEXT STEPS

Test comprehension before expanding the feature set.

The next step would be short task-based sessions with legal reviewers. Starting at the workspace, I would ask them to locate both source clauses, explain the conflict and decide what they would inspect before accepting the proposed amendment. I would observe where they look and what information they ask for.

I would then check whether they distinguish AI suggestions from human comments, predict what Reject does, and recover an accepted amendment using Undo. At the summary, I would ask what they believe happens next and whether the document is already approved.

Task completion, missed source checks, mistaken state interpretation and recovery attempts would guide the next iteration. Questions about source authority and acceptable legal wording would require domain input before expanding this into a broader product workflow.
