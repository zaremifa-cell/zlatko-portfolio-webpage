01 — CONTEXT & PROBLEM

A discrepancy is easy to flag. A decision needs context.

This concept explores a narrow part of fund-document review: what happens after AI identifies conflicting wording. The interface needs to help a reviewer understand the finding, inspect the evidence and make an accountable decision. Flagging a sentence alone does not explain which source should guide the amendment.

The example uses Northgate Growth Fund III, a fictional fund. Clause 3.1 of its term sheet states a ten-year duration; Article 2.1 of its draft Limited Partnership Agreement (LPA) states eight years. For this scenario, the term sheet is treated as the agreed reference. That is an explicit scenario assumption, not a rule that a term sheet always takes precedence.

The problem hypothesis is that switching between documents, AI suggestions and comments makes it harder to keep the source of a decision visible. The proposed workspace brings these elements together while preserving their different roles. Hypotheses based on public material on fund documentation; not validated with users.

02 — REVIEWER & SCOPE

One reviewer, one conflict, one complete handoff.

The assumed primary user is a legal associate preparing a draft for partner review. Their immediate task is to resolve a duration discrepancy without losing the original wording, its reference or the history of the amendment. In the fictional scenario, E. Markova reviews the draft, Daniel Reed provides investor-counsel feedback and John Hale is the approving partner. M. Chen owns a different fund in the document list.

The project concentrates on one task: open the document, inspect the finding, compare the two clauses, accept or edit the proposed amendment, review the summary and send the draft for approval. Keeping this scope makes the consequences of each action easier to demonstrate in a short prototype.

Document upload, clause generation, permissions, fund onboarding and a complete legal-review workflow are outside this prototype. The deliverable is a focused interaction proposal supported by reusable Figma components. All funds, people, firms, clauses, source excerpts and reviewer records in the concept are fictional. The example has not been legally reviewed.

03 — INFORMATION HIERARCHY

Keep the document central and the finding in context.

The workspace uses three areas: section navigation on the left, the document in the middle and a review panel on the right. The document remains the main reading surface. Navigation gives the reviewer a location within it; the panel explains why the selected clause needs attention.

The selected Article 2.1 connects the document text to the finding. The AI card identifies the duration conflict and points toward the term sheet reference. A separate human comment includes an author and firm, so a reader can distinguish a generated suggestion from a person’s feedback before acting.

This is the concept’s proposed arrangement. It is intended to reduce the amount of context the reviewer has to remember while moving between a clause and its feedback. That benefit remains a hypothesis until the layout is tested with reviewers.

04 — SOURCE COMPARISON

Show what disagrees before asking for acceptance.

The comparison step places the term sheet clause and the LPA clause together. The important information is specific: where each excerpt comes from, what duration it states and which wording would change. The reviewer should be able to explain the discrepancy without relying on the AI label alone.

The message “Conflicts with term sheet · Review required” describes the basis of the finding. A confidence percentage was deliberately left out: a high number would not establish that the source is applicable or that the proposed wording is legally appropriate.

Accept, Edit and Reject sit beside the proposed amendment. Accept applies the prepared duration change. Edit demonstrates a reviewer-adjusted version of the wording. The rejection form starts with a placeholder instead of an AI-written explanation. In the intended interaction, the reviewer enters a reason before confirming. Reject records the reviewer’s decision and leaves the issue open, because declining one suggestion does not resolve the underlying disagreement.

05 — DECISIONS & RECOVERY

Make the change traceable and recoverable.

After acceptance, the interface records the reviewer and time alongside the amendment. Added and Removed appear as text labels as well as colored indicators. This makes the meaning of the change explicit and avoids depending on color alone.

A saved-change notice is visible for ten seconds. The single Undo acceptance action stays in the decision history. When the notice disappears, the accepted decision stays accepted; using Undo restores the finding to Open.

The accepted card prominently names E. Markova as the person who accepted the amendment, while retaining the AI origin label. The prototype separates an AI suggestion’s state from the document’s approval state. A suggestion can be accepted while the document remains a draft. This distinction carries through to the summary and final confirmation, where the draft is still awaiting partner review.

06 — APPROVAL HANDOFF

Summarize the decision before sending the draft.

The summary gives the reviewer a final check of accepted changes and open findings, with a short note for the partner. In the main scenario there is one accepted amendment and zero open findings. Those counts describe the demonstrated review task; they do not establish that the entire document is legally correct.

Send for approval leads to a confirmation that names the partner and says the document is awaiting review. This closes the demonstrated task while keeping the next responsibility visible. The action is simulated: the prototype does not send a real document or notification.

The confirmation is a prototype state attached to the approval flow, rather than a sixth core screen. Demonstration controls are kept out of the product interface. The prototype does not maintain persistent document data.

07 — FIGMA FOUNDATIONS

Build the states from a small, editable system.

The file separates Foundations, Components and Screens. Five core screens form the main flow; editing, rejection and confirmation are supporting prototype states. Its foundations include 52 primitive and semantic variables and seven text styles. Semantic roles distinguish page backgrounds, surfaces, primary and secondary text, borders, actions and review statuses, so the screens share the same visual decisions.

Five component families cover buttons, status tags, diff chips, human comment cards and AI suggestion cards. Across 29 variants, these components express changes in size, emphasis and state. Auto Layout, component instances and variable bindings keep the deliverable editable rather than flattening it into screenshots.

The neutral document surface supports sustained reading, while a restrained accent identifies actions. AI cards have an explicit label and a left-side marker; human comments carry authorship. The purpose of these choices is to make the source and meaning of an item legible without adding competing decoration.

08 — PROCESS & CHECKS

Be precise about what was made and what was tested.

Claude (Anthropic) supported the initial scope, UX copy and public-research draft supplied by the author. The author reports reading Hibernia pages as text through WebFetch and seeing only FundLife’s landing-page title and description at that stage. The competitive research has not been independently verified. During implementation, Codex generated editable Figma layers, components and variables through a write-capable Figma tool, then applied further corrections in the browser editor. Codex also exercised the main prototype journey in Chrome. These implementation checks were performed by the assistant, not by the author, and are not user research.

The main journey was exercised in Figma presentation mode from the document list through the approval-request confirmation. Prototype destinations were checked structurally, and composed screens were inspected for clipping. These checks establish that the demonstrated path is connected; they do not establish that legal reviewers would understand or prefer it.

Free-form editing is represented by prepared text, submission is simulated and the document examples are fictional. There are no claims of faster review, increased trust or business impact. The outcome of this exercise is an inspectable design proposal and a working demonstration of its main task.

09 — NEXT STEPS

Test comprehension before expanding the feature set.

The next step would be short task-based sessions with legal reviewers. Starting at the workspace, I would ask them to locate both source clauses, explain the conflict and decide what they would inspect before accepting the proposed amendment. I would observe where they look and what information they ask for.

I would then check whether they distinguish AI suggestions from human comments, predict what Reject does, and recover an accepted amendment using Undo. At the summary, I would ask what they believe happens next and whether the document is already approved.

Task completion, missed source checks, mistaken state interpretation and recovery attempts would guide the next iteration. Questions about source authority and acceptable legal wording would require domain input before expanding this into a broader product workflow.
