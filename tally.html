PRODUCT DESIGN + REACT — AI AGENT SPEND

A concept for the screen engineering leads open when the AI bill matters: how much coding agents are spending, which team is about to hit its cap, which key has gone rogue, and how much routing to open models is saving — in the browser and in the terminal.

Four questions. One screen each.

INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
  1. 01 / HOW MUCHOverview

    Month to date, forecast at the current pace, savings from routing, open alerts.

  2. 02 / WHOTeams & keys

    Every team against its cap, every key with its last 72 hours, one click to pause.

  3. 03 / WHAT WENT WRONGAlerts

    Each alert carries its evidence chart and the action that resolves it.

  4. 04 / FROM THE TERMINALCLI

    The same numbers as commands, for the people who live in a terminal.

All figures come from 45 days of generated hourly usage for 5 teams, 18 keys and 6 models, including one runaway CI agent. Totals always add up because every number is derived from the same records.

01 — THE PROBLEM

Agents spend money while nobody is watching.

A coding agent is a loop with a credit card. Most of the time it is cheap; when it gets stuck retrying on an expensive model it can burn a week of budget in an afternoon, often at night. Platform teams need to see three things fast: are we on track for the month, which team is about to be stopped by its cap, and which key is behaving unlike itself.

02 — DECISIONS

Every number has to earn its place.

  • Compare a key with itselfAn anomaly is spend above 4× the same key’s usual rate for that hour of day, two hours in a row. Morning ramp-ups and one big refactor are not alarms.
  • Forecast from the last 7 daysAdoption grows week to week; a month average would forecast too low and warn too late.
  • Caps as burn-upCumulative spend against the cap, with the forecast dashed and a ring where it crosses: the row answers “will we hit it?”.
  • Brightness, not redSeverity is carried by contrast and inverted tags; colour is reserved for “on”. Calm by default, unmistakable when it matters.
  • Readable next to a spikeThe hourly chart uses a square-root scale with euro labels, so normal hours stay visible beside a 15× spike.
  • Real, not decorativeThe barcode is a scannable Code 128 report number and the QR code opens this page.

03 — VISUAL LANGUAGE

Two references, one system.

Frosted, matte glass carries navigation, status strips and tables over a blurred photographic backdrop; white hairline “ticket” frames with a rotated spine, light grotesk numerals and boxed labels carry the headline figures and the charts. Type is limited to a heavy display wordmark, one mono size for labels and one for controls. Crop marks sit on the extensions of the content edges at equal distances. Every chart is drawn by hand in SVG at its real pixel width, so axis labels stay legible on a phone.

04 — WHAT I WOULD RESEARCH NEXT

A concept is a set of hypotheses.

This is a concept built without access to real users, so the decisions above are hypotheses. The research I would run first, with platform engineers and engineering managers who pay for coding agents:

  • How do teams set caps today, and who is allowed to raise one when work is blocked?
  • What makes a spend alert trustworthy enough to act on at night — and what makes people mute it?
  • Do engineers want to see cost while they work in the CLI, or only after the fact?
  • Is “saved by routing” convincing, or does it need a quality comparison next to it?

Method: five to eight 30-minute interviews, a walkthrough of each participant’s real invoice or usage export, and a task-based test of the alert-to-pause flow on this prototype.

05 — BOUNDARIES

Say what it is.

Synthetic data and illustrative model prices; no real billing or provider APIs are connected, and pausing a key only changes the prototype’s state. Not affiliated with any vendor. Built with AI-assisted tooling; the design decisions are mine and documented in the repository.
